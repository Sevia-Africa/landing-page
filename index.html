Why Sevia Exists

Good Work Exists. The Connections Often Don't.

Across Africa, women are already building.

They are teaching, trading, organizing communities, creating businesses, developing technology, conducting research, translating knowledge, supporting families, and solving problems that formal systems often overlook.

But much of this work exists in fragments.

One community may have the knowledge another community needs. One woman may have the skills to solve a problem, while another has the relationships or resources to make that solution possible. A successful local initiative may remain local simply because there is no pathway for its knowledge, tools, or experience to travel.

This fragmentation creates gaps. It can lead to duplicated effort, disconnected resources, limited access to mentorship and opportunity, and solutions that are difficult to sustain or replicate. It can also make collaboration harder and prevent promising ideas from developing beyond their original communities.

The problem is not always a lack of people doing the work. Sometimes, the missing infrastructure is the connection between them.

Sevia exists to help create those connections. We connect people, knowledge, tools, resources, partnerships, and opportunities so that useful work can move further than the place where it began.

A lesson can become a playbook.
A translation can become shared infrastructure.
A local initiative can find a regional collaborator.
A contribution can lead to an opportunity.
A community solution can become something others can build on.

Sevia is designed to strengthen the connections between what already exists — while helping communities build what is still missing.

We are not starting from nothing. We are connecting what is already being built and creating the infrastructure that helps it travel.

Collaboration

Sovereignty

Innovation

We build openly and adapt as the network learns, rather than treating any single tool or approach as permanent.

What Are We Building?

The Infrastructure That Helps Contributions Connect.

Sevia is not one organization trying to do everything. It is a coordination and infrastructure layer that helps different forms of contribution connect and become more useful to one another.

Someone contributes knowledge. Someone contributes code. Someone contributes funding. Someone contributes research. Someone contributes time. Someone contributes a relationship. Someone contributes access to a community.

Individually, these contributions can be valuable. Connected, they can become infrastructure.

The Sevia Flywheel

The Sevia flywheel represents how contributions can move through the network and strengthen one another.

Knowledge & Time → Advisory Network

People contribute knowledge, experience, mentorship, and time. These contributions create an advisory layer that helps others make better decisions, solve problems, and develop their capacity.

Research & Data → Open Insights

Local research and community knowledge are documented and shared so that others can understand what is happening on the ground and build from evidence rather than assumptions.

Code & Tools → Transparency Hub

Builders can contribute open-source tools, technical systems, and reusable infrastructure that make contributions, projects, and resources easier to discover and coordinate.

Logistics & Assets → Shared Supply

Physical resources, operational capacity, venues, equipment, networks, and other assets can be coordinated where they are useful to communities and initiatives.

Advocacy & Policy → Market Access

Knowledge, relationships, and collective representation can help women navigate markets, institutions, and policy environments that may otherwise remain difficult to access.

Grant & Capital → Growth Fund

Capital can help promising contributions, community initiatives, pilots, research, and infrastructure move from ideas into working projects.

Community → Local Cooperatives

People organize locally around shared needs and opportunities, creating community-level structures that can become more resilient and increasingly self-sustaining.

These are not isolated departments operating independently. They feed one another.

Research can inform education. Education can create builders. Builders can create tools. Tools can strengthen communities. Communities can generate new research. Funding can help promising ideas scale. Mentorship can turn contributors into leaders. And the cycle continues.

The goal is not to build everything ourselves. The goal is to make it easier for what people build to strengthen what others are building.

How Does Sevia Work?

Connect People. Share Knowledge. Build Together.

Sevia works by creating pathways between people, communities, knowledge, tools, resources, and opportunities. It starts with contribution.

People enter the network with something they already know, do, have, or can connect. That contribution may be education, mentorship, translation, research, code, community organizing, funding, introductions, operational support, technical expertise, creative work, or local knowledge.

Sevia helps document, organize, connect, and route those contributions where they can create value.

1

Contribute

You bring what you already have. You do not need to be an expert in everything or take on the entire mission. You contribute one useful piece.

2

Connect

Sevia creates pathways between contributors, communities, projects, mentors, partners, funders, and existing ecosystems — meaningful connections around real work, not just a directory of people.

3

Share

Useful knowledge, research, playbooks, translations, tools, and lessons can be documented and made reusable, so a solution developed in one place can benefit another community.

4

Build

Connected people can collaborate on projects, tools, community initiatives, educational resources, economic experiments, and infrastructure. Where appropriate, Sevia helps provide the coordination layer around that work.

5

Multiply

A contributor can become a mentor. A workshop can become a curriculum. A local solution can become a regional model. A relationship can open a new opportunity. A project can create new contributors.

This is how the network grows without requiring everything to remain dependent on one central team.

Useful knowledge shouldn't stop where it starts. A workshop in Kenya can inform a guide in Tanzania. A local translation can open doors across borders. One community solution becomes a shared foundation for many. Open pathways for cross-border collaboration among African women leaders.

Partnerships

Sevia also works with aligned organizations, open-source projects, technical ecosystems, funders, educators, community initiatives, and other partners. We do not assume Sevia needs to build every underlying tool itself.

Where strong infrastructure already exists, Sevia can connect communities to it. Where gaps remain, the network can help identify, coordinate, and build what is missing.

Sevia is the connection layer. The people and communities remain the builders.

Why Bitcoin?

Why Bitcoin Is Part of the Sevia Infrastructure

Bitcoin

An open monetary network that gives people another way to store and move value.

Lightning

A faster, lower-cost way to make Bitcoin payments.

Open Source

Technology that communities can inspect, improve, adapt, and build on.

Bitcoin is not the entirety of Sevia. It is one of the open systems that Sevia builds with.

For Sevia, Bitcoin matters because it provides an open monetary network that people can access without needing permission from a single institution, company, or country. Its usefulness goes beyond holding an asset — it creates infrastructure that can support new ways of saving, transacting, building, and coordinating economic activity.

Open Monetary Network

Bitcoin's underlying protocol is not controlled by one bank or company, allowing people across different countries to participate in the same monetary network. For African communities operating across borders, this creates possibilities not limited entirely by national financial systems.

Self-Custody

Bitcoin can be held directly by the person using it. Self-custody gives individuals control over their own funds rather than relying entirely on an intermediary. For Sevia, this makes financial education inseparable from security, backups, privacy, and operational safety. Access without understanding is not enough.

Peer-to-Peer Transactions

Bitcoin enables people to transact directly with one another — useful for individuals, traders, community initiatives, and businesses that need practical ways to receive or send value. The objective is not to replace every existing financial system, but to give people additional tools and greater choice.

Lightning

The Lightning Network can enable faster, lower-cost Bitcoin payments — making Bitcoin more practical for everyday transactions, including merchant payments, community economies, and cross-border peer-to-peer activity.

Open-Source Infrastructure

Bitcoin's ecosystem is supported by open-source software and protocols that developers and communities can inspect, improve, adapt, and build on. Build openly where possible. Share what is useful. Let others build further.

Privacy

Financial participation can expose people to risks that differ from one community to another, so Bitcoin cannot be approached only as a payment technology. Privacy, identity protection, secure communication, and responsible custody matter. Sevia connects Bitcoin education with these realities rather than treating technical access as the end goal.

Financial Sovereignty

For Sevia, financial sovereignty means increasing people's ability to understand, control, and participate in the systems through which they save, transact, build, and exchange value. Bitcoin is one tool within that larger objective.

Practical African Use Cases

Cross-border payments, merchant payments, peer-to-peer transactions, savings and value preservation, community economies, remittances, financial education, open-source technology development, and economic coordination across borders.

Sevia focuses on practical use, informed participation, and responsible experimentation rather than speculation or hype.

We start with knowledge. We build the capacity to use the tools responsibly. And from there, people can move from learning to using, building, and leading.

What This Looks Like

From One Local Initiative to a Connected Network

Ama is a fictional example used to illustrate how participation in the Sevia network can develop over time.

1

Discovery & Learning

Ama joins a localized workshop in Kumasi powered by Sevia's open-source educational playbooks. She accesses localized guides translated by community contributors into Twi and begins learning about digital security, Bitcoin, and self-custody. She is not simply given a tool — she develops the knowledge to understand how the tool works and how to use it responsibly.

2

Receives Mentorship

As her interest develops, Ama connects with technical mentors across the network. With guidance, she begins exploring how a community-run Lightning node could support practical payments and economic activity in her community — learning not only how the technology works, but how to think about security, operations, and community responsibility.

3

Micro-Contribution & Earning

Ama notices that an existing merchant onboarding guide could be more useful to Hausa-speaking communities. She translates it into Hausa. Her contribution is reviewed and published to the Sevia repository, becoming part of shared infrastructure. She receives recognition and is connected to a borderless peer-to-peer funding opportunity that supports her continued work.

4

Collaboration & Building

Through the network, Ama meets a designer in Nairobi and a community organizer in Lagos. Together, they develop a privacy-conscious local savings-circle model, drawing on existing open tools and adapting the approach to their communities.

5

Builds Capacity & Leads

Ama's experience grows. She launches a local cooperative and begins training more than 50 market women on Bitcoin self-custody, security, and practical use. She is no longer only a participant — she is becoming a local source of knowledge and capacity.

6

Leadership & Multiplication

Ama eventually mentors 15 women in her region. She has developed the relationships, knowledge, tools, and confidence to operate independently — continuing to build through open infrastructure, trusted relationships, and the wider network, without needing Sevia's central team to authorize every decision.

7

She Earns Income

Ama's growing expertise becomes paid work — leading paid workshops, coordinating grants for other contributors, and eventually developing her own product line built on what she has learned. Her contribution has become a livelihood, not just a project.

8

Regional Leadership

Ama is voted into Sevia's Regional Representation layer, coordinating with elected leaders from other regions on shared priorities, resource stewardship, and network-wide decisions. She has moved from learning, to earning, to leading — and is now shaping the infrastructure she once relied on.

This is the point. Sevia should not become the organization everyone depends on. It should help people develop the capacity, connections, and infrastructure to become increasingly capable of building without it.

Our Origin Story

Sevia Is Built by People, Not a Single Founder.

Sevia is built on existing open tools, ecosystems, relationships, and guardrails. We do not need to rebuild everything ourselves. We invite aligned people and organizations to help strengthen what already exists and build what is still missing.

Origin

Across Africa, women are among the most important economic actors in their families and communities. They trade. They build businesses. They manage household economies. They support children and extended families. They create informal systems of savings, trade, care, and resilience.

Yet the women carrying much of this economic activity often have the least access to the systems designed to protect and expand it — facing financial exclusion, currency instability, expensive cross-border transfers, limited access to formal financial services, and insufficient access to modern digital tools and infrastructure.

At the same time, women already building solutions often remain disconnected from one another, from technical expertise, from capital, and from wider ecosystem opportunities.

Sevia began with a simple question: what could become possible if the women already building across Africa had stronger connections to one another, to open infrastructure, and to the opportunities around them? That question became Sevia Africa.

Founding Stewardship

Sevia began through the stewardship of Sena Ami, who initiated the vision and is responsible for helping establish its foundational infrastructure, relationships, governance systems, contribution pathways, and early ecosystem development.

The founding steward's role is not to become the permanent center of Sevia. It is to help establish the conditions for a network that can eventually operate through distributed leadership. As Sevia grows, responsibility should increasingly be shared across contributors, leaders, working groups, regional nodes, and the wider ecosystem.

Leadership

Sevia's leadership is designed to develop through contribution. A woman does not need to lead every part of the network — she can lead where her knowledge, experience, relationships, or capabilities are strongest.

A technical contributor may lead technical work. A researcher may lead research. An educator may lead learning initiatives. A community organizer may lead regional activation. A woman with experience in finance may help guide treasury or capital systems.

These areas of leadership are not meant to compete with one another. They compound toward a unified goal. The strength of Sevia comes from bringing different expertise, perspectives, regions, and lived experiences together without requiring everyone to become the same kind of leader.

Sevia is designed to grow beyond any one founder, with no single point of failure.

Governance

Leadership That Is Shared, Distributed, and Accountable.

Sevia is designed to grow through shared stewardship rather than permanent dependence on one person or central team. Governance exists to protect the mission, distribute responsibility, create accountability, and ensure that leadership remains connected to the communities and work the network exists to support.

Steering / Sovereignty Council

Provides strategic stewardship for Sevia, helping guide strategic direction, ecosystem relationships, major initiatives, standards and safeguards, resource stewardship, regional development, and long-term resilience. Council members should bring meaningful expertise and represent different parts of the ecosystem rather than simply holding titles.

Regional Representation

Sevia is pan-African. Its leadership needs perspectives from different regions and realities across the continent, preventing the network from being shaped by a single country's assumptions or priorities. The goal is not geographical representation for its own sake — it is contextual intelligence.

Working Groups

Specialized working groups can develop around Education, Advocacy & Policy, Technology & Tools, Community, Finance & Economic Opportunity, Research, Security & Privacy, and other areas that emerge from the network's needs.

Rotational Leadership

Leadership should not become permanently concentrated. Where appropriate, leadership responsibilities can rotate on a defined cycle, creating opportunities for emerging leaders and helping distribute institutional knowledge.

Accountability

Participants entrusted with governance roles should operate according to agreed standards around transparency, conflicts of interest, responsible stewardship, confidentiality, security, respectful conduct, and appropriate use of community resources.

Participation Standards

Participation should be based on contribution, integrity, respect, and alignment with Sevia's principles. Being part of the network should not automatically mean having access to every system, resource, or decision — access should correspond to role, responsibility, trust, and risk.

Decision-Making

Sevia should use the simplest decision-making process appropriate to the decision. Local contributors retain autonomy where possible; working groups can decide within their agreed scope; major strategic, financial, governance, or security decisions have stronger review and accountability mechanisms.

Stewardship

Stewardship means protecting the network for the people who depend on it — responsible handling of community resources, funding, data, infrastructure, relationships, reputation, and security. Where funds require collective control, Sevia can use distributed stewardship and appropriate multisignature structures.

Good governance should make Sevia stronger without making it heavier.

Trust and Protection

Participation Without Unnecessary Exposure.

Sevia is designed to support different levels of visibility, privacy, and operational risk. The network should not assume that every woman can participate publicly in the same way.

The Framework

Identity-Aware Participation

Different contexts require different approaches to identity. Participation can be designed around the actual risk involved.

Privacy-Conscious Communication

Use communication practices and tools that reduce unnecessary exposure of personal information.

Public vs Protected Participation

Some contributors may participate openly. Others may require limited attribution or protected participation.

Non-Custodial Principles

Where appropriate, individuals and communities should retain control of their own assets rather than unnecessarily transferring control to Sevia.

Distributed Stewardship

Critical responsibilities should not depend unnecessarily on one person.

Multisig Structures

Community funds or shared resources can use multisignature arrangements where appropriate.

Operational Security

People participating in sensitive environments need practical security knowledge, not simply technical tools.

Existing Ecosystem Guardrails

Sevia should build on established security, privacy, custody, communication, and infrastructure practices wherever appropriate rather than reinventing them.

Who Can Access What?

Access to information and systems should be based on role, responsibility, necessity, and risk.

How Contributor Information Is Handled
Sevia should minimize unnecessary collection, clearly define how information is used, and respect contributor privacy preferences.

Contribution to the Framework

OpSec & Safety Guides

Help write or translate threat models and operational security guides for grassroots organizers.

Privacy & Tech Guardrails

Help audit or improve data-minimization practices, decentralized identity tools, and encrypted communication setups.

Peer-to-Peer Support

We also provide a protected contribution channel — a clear pathway for contributors to share feedback, concerns, or threat assessments without requiring unnecessary public identification.

Step 3 of 5 — Privacy & Participation

Your Participation. Your Choice.

Sevia is built to be open, but openness should never require unnecessary exposure. You decide how you would like to participate and how your contribution should be represented.

Your name or chosen identity and contribution may be publicly associated with your participation.

Your contribution may be recognized within relevant Sevia circles, while limiting what personal information is shared publicly.

Your participation is handled with greater privacy and limited attribution based on your preferences and the nature of your contribution.

Step 4 of 5 — Information & Protection

We ask for information to understand your contribution, communicate with you, and determine the most appropriate way for you to participate. We aim to collect only what is useful for these purposes.

Your information should not automatically become public simply because you contribute to an open network. Your contribution can be open without your personal information being open.

You do not need to publicly identify yourself to tell us how you can contribute. Your contribution matters. Your safety matters too.

Grant & Capital

Fund the Infrastructure

Capital can help promising contributions, community initiatives, pilots, research, and infrastructure move from ideas into working projects. Fund what strengthens the network, not just a single organization.

Funding at Sevia is treated as one contribution among many — not the only one that matters. A dollar of capital and an hour of mentorship can strengthen the same piece of infrastructure in different ways.

We're looking for funders who want their capital to reach specific, useful work — education, translation, open-source tools, community pilots, research, and the security and operational capacity that keeps contributors safe.

What Funding Can Support

Education

Workshops, curricula, and localized learning materials for communities across the network.

Translation

Making existing knowledge and tools accessible across more African languages.

Open-Source Development

Builders creating and maintaining shared tools, protocols, and technical infrastructure.

Community Pilots

Early-stage local initiatives testing new approaches before they scale.

Research

Documenting local realities, economic patterns, and emerging opportunities.

Security & Operational Capacity

The privacy, protection, and operational security that make participation possible for more women.

How Funding Is Stewarded

Where funding mechanisms are operational, we aim to route capital through transparent processes with appropriate reporting — not as a black box. Where appropriate, individuals and communities retain control of their own resources rather than unnecessarily transferring control to Sevia, and shared or pooled funds can use distributed stewardship and multisignature structures rather than depending on one person.

Code & Tools

Transparency Hub

Open tools and technical systems that make contributions, projects, and resources easier to discover, coordinate, and trust.

Sevia is meant to be a network people can trust without having to take it on faith. That means being clear about how decisions get made, how resources are stewarded, and what standards participants are held to — while still respecting that not everyone can, or should, be visible in the same way.

Visibility should never be a requirement for participation. Transparency at Sevia is about the systems and decisions, not about exposing individual contributors who need privacy or protection.

SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

The Contribution Layer Breakdown

Detailed overview of how individual contribution channels interconnect to power Sevia's decentralized infrastructure:

1. Education & Language Translation

Transforms technical open-source playbooks and Bitcoin self-custody modules into regional languages, expanding baseline access across indigenous communities.

2. Mentorship & Capacity Building

Connects experienced leaders with emerging builders to accelerate technical skills, operational resilience, and non-custodial financial practices.

3. Technical Development & Tools

Develops permissionless protocols, privacy-focused toolsets, and open code repositories owned and maintained by the ecosystem.

4. Local Grassroots Hubs

Establishes physical and digital meeting spaces for peer-to-peer resource sharing, localized trade networks, and security workshops.

5. Non-Custodial Capital & Funding

Directs peer-to-peer micro-grants and community funding pools straight to local infrastructure projects without centralized intermediaries.
